Designing REMNANT

Echoes of Memory

How we connected computational puzzles, six changes of pace and a robot's search for human memory.

In REMNANT, we put a robot in a world that has survived its people. NOVA follows a program to rebuild Earth, while scattered memories gradually reveal what happened to humanity. The game asks what a machine should preserve when it reconstructs a human world.

We also wanted players to encounter computer science through action. A broken facility gave us a setting for that: routing signals, interpreting binary clues and changing the state of a room all become ways to move the story forward. The resulting game has six chapters, with a different kind of interaction in each.

A machine's orders, a human's memories

The story follows two lines. The machine side offers structure: an assistant provides hints, tasks state an objective, and driving assistance can help the player follow a route. These systems make order useful. The player has reasons to trust them.

Memory fragments complicate that trust. They reveal NOVA's identity and the human experiences hidden inside the robot's memory. Searching for them gives players a reason to explore beyond the obvious route. Progress through the world and recovery of the past happen together.

The final decision brings the two lines into conflict. Restoring human memories preserves emotion and the freedom to choose. Continuing as the Judicator prioritizes order and the reconstruction of Earth. We wanted the earlier interactions to give that decision some weight.

The two endings (spoilers)

Six chapters, six changes of pace

The changes in movement are part of the narrative rhythm. Driving gives the player time to look around. The laboratory slows the pace and asks for careful reasoning. Grappling and combat then demand quicker decisions. Flight opens up the space again, giving the memory-restoration ending a sense of release.

Keeping the route legible

Glass partitions reveal the destination while the actual route takes a detour. The two-floor structure also lets us create a shortcut: after solving a puzzle, the player can drop from the upper level to the lower one. The space becomes easier to traverse as the player understands it. We placed the binary hint before the maze entrance, where it can inform the next decision.

The room also needs to show what a solution changes. Restoring a circuit opens a door or removes a barrier, so progress on a puzzle becomes a visible change in the environment. This connects the layout, its visual cues and the player's actions.

Inside Level 3: the laboratory

One of REMNANT's distinctive features is its laboratory chapter, where computer science concepts become puzzles in a 3D space. Five puzzle areas ask the player to reason about different kinds of systems: a connected path, competing routes, a signal network, a maze with conditional doors, and a floor of interacting binary states. Solving them restores the facility and changes where NOVA can go.

01 · Pipes: finding the path that carries power

The pipe board has 25 cells. Players rotate segments to carry power from the input to the output, then use Enable Circuit to test the route. Some pieces are decoys: making them look connected contributes nothing to the powered path.

That distinction gives the puzzle its purpose. The player has to trace which components belong to the working system and check their orientation. Local connections only help when they form a continuous route. Completing the circuit unlocks the physical way forward in the chamber, tying the result on the panel to movement through the room.

02 · Wires: several routes sharing one board

The wire puzzle asks players to connect matching colored terminals across a limited grid. A route that works on its own can occupy the only corridor another wire needs. Players often have to reconsider an earlier connection to make the whole layout work.

We drew on the layout problem in printed circuit boards. Every required connection has to coexist with the others. The interface tracks progress through successive routing stages; glowing paths show completed connections, and Reset lets the player try another arrangement. Completing the connections drives the room's door-opening animation.

03 · Logic gates: building a valid signal network

Our gate puzzle combines three signal states, red, blue and white, with a limited inventory of five operations. Players arrange gates and wires in columns. Signals move from left to right through every column; an empty cell can carry a wire, while an occupied gate cell blocks unrelated routes.

We implemented the gate logic in C++. Run checks the target colors together with the wiring rules. It rejects a two-input gate with only one input, a wire that skips a column, or a SPLIT output left unconnected. Every generated signal must have somewhere to go. Once the circuit passes validation, the laser barrier clears.

SPLIT is the only legal way to branch a signal. A gate with two inputs consumes two signals and produces one; SWAP leaves the count unchanged. With no unused outputs, a valid board must satisfy:

Targets = sources + SPLIT gates − two-input gates

Two sources and three targets therefore require one more SPLIT than merging gate. This gives the player a way to rule out an impossible design before routing every wire. The remaining task is to achieve the target colors within the available columns and gate inventory.

04 · The maze: decoding an instruction, then judging a door

A binary message on the wall becomes an ordered sequence of door numbers. The example route in our guide is 2 → 1 → 3 → 2 → 3. Entering the maze turns that decoded instruction into a spatial problem: the player has to find a usable door for each step.

We gave the doors six behaviors. They make the player check whether a route is actually available, and from which direction. A room can be understood as a graph node; a door adds an edge with a condition, a direction or a changing label.

Two-way
Lets the player return and recheck a junction.

The clue supplies an instruction sequence; observation decides how to carry it out. A matching number on a fake door still leaves the player looking for a valid passage. That is where binary decoding meets conditional reasoning and graph traversal.

05 · Lights Out: an algorithm you can walk into

In the light room, the player operates a floor of 25 blocks. Pressing one reverses its state and the states of its horizontal and vertical neighbors. Correcting an orange block can undo a nearby cyan one, so each move has to account for the surrounding pattern.

We map the blocks' 3D positions to their grid coordinates and broadcast state changes to the affected neighbors. The immediate change in color lets the player test the rule in place. Cyan is the target state; restoring all 25 panels completes the mechanism.

This is a classic algorithmic puzzle: each press is a binary decision, and the local flip rule allows a small search over the first row to determine the entire solution. Our contribution is its place in the laboratory: a familiar algorithm becomes a floor the player can explore, test and restore.

1. State the problem precisely

The light floor is an instance of Lights Out, a classic algorithmic puzzle. The project guide describes the same first-row enumeration used below [1]. This notebook develops that idea into a solver; the Python is a standalone explanation of the rule, not the Unreal Engine implementation.

Consider an m×nm\times nm×n rectangular board. A press flips its own cell and its orthogonal neighbors. Diagonals are unchanged, and the edges do not wrap around. A corner press changes three cells, an edge press four, and an interior press five on our 5×55\times55×5 board.

Write the initial state as sr,c∈{0,1}s_{r,c}\in\{0,1\}sr,c​∈{0,1}: cyan is 111, orange is 000. REMNANT asks for all lights on. The usual all-off convention becomes the same problem if we work with the mismatch from the target:

dr,c=sr,c⊕tr,c,tr,c=1.d_{r,c}=s_{r,c}\oplus t_{r,c},\qquad t_{r,c}=1. dr,c​=sr,c​⊕tr,c​,tr,c​=1.

Here ⊕\oplus⊕ means XOR, or addition modulo two. A mismatch of 111 means that a cell needs an odd number of flips. A mismatch of 000 needs an even number, including zero. Our task is to find a press pattern that removes every mismatch; a second task is to use as few presses as possible.

2. Replace action sequences with binary decisions

Two presses on the same cell cancel. The order of presses also makes no difference, because XOR is associative and commutative:

a⊕1⊕1=a,a⊕b⊕c=a⊕c⊕b.\begin{aligned} a\oplus1\oplus1&=a,\\ a\oplus b\oplus c&=a\oplus c\oplus b. \end{aligned} a⊕1⊕1a⊕b⊕c​=a,=a⊕c⊕b.​

We can therefore describe a solution by pr,c∈{0,1}p_{r,c}\in\{0,1\}pr,c​∈{0,1}, recording whether a cell is pressed once. There is no need to search through all possible orders or repeated actions. The final state is

sr,c′=sr,c⊕pr,c⊕pr−1,c⊕pr+1,c⊕pr,c−1⊕pr,c+1.\begin{aligned} s'_{r,c}={}&s_{r,c}\oplus p_{r,c}\oplus p_{r-1,c}\\ &\oplus p_{r+1,c}\oplus p_{r,c-1}\oplus p_{r,c+1}. \end{aligned} sr,c′​=​sr,c​⊕pr,c​⊕pr−1,c​⊕pr+1,c​⊕pr,c−1​⊕pr,c+1​.​

All press variables outside the board are zero. Setting sr,c′=tr,cs'_{r,c}=t_{r,c}sr,c′​=tr,c​ gives one parity constraint for each cell. A direct enumeration still has 2mn2^{mn}2mn candidate press patterns: for 5×55\times55×5, that is 225=33,554,4322^{25}=33{,}554{,}432225=33,554,432. The grid structure lets us do much better.

3. Guess the first row, derive the rest

Choose the presses in row 1. Once those choices are fixed, inspect that row: every orange cell must be flipped by pressing the cell directly below it. Among presses in later rows, that is the only press that can still change this cell. Pressing in row 3 or below cannot reach row 1.

Use row 2 to finish row 1, row 3 to finish row 2, and so on. This is often called light chasing. In terms of the original mismatch and the press decisions, its recurrence is

pr+1,c=dr,c⊕pr−1,c⊕pr,c⊕pr,c−1⊕pr,c+1,r<m.\begin{aligned} p_{r+1,c}={}&d_{r,c}\oplus p_{r-1,c}\oplus p_{r,c}\\ &\oplus p_{r,c-1}\oplus p_{r,c+1},\qquad r<m. \end{aligned} pr+1,c​=​dr,c​⊕pr−1,c​⊕pr,c​⊕pr,c−1​⊕pr,c+1​,r<m.​

The same zero boundary convention applies, including p0,c=0p_{0,c}=0p0,c​=0. This equation uses the initial mismatch ddd. In a mutable-board implementation, the equivalent instruction is simply: if the cell above is currently orange, press below it.

There is no row beneath row mmm. After chasing, the last row must already match the target. Equivalently, every column must satisfy

dm,c⊕pm−1,c⊕pm,c⊕pm,c−1⊕pm,c+1=0.\begin{aligned} &d_{m,c}\oplus p_{m-1,c}\oplus p_{m,c}\\ &\qquad\oplus p_{m,c-1}\oplus p_{m,c+1}=0. \end{aligned} ​dm,c​⊕pm−1,c​⊕pm,c​⊕pm,c−1​⊕pm,c+1​=0.​

A failed last row rejects the chosen first-row pattern. It does not justify pressing arbitrary cells in the last row again, since that would disturb the row above. For five columns, we only need to try 25=322^5=3225=32 first-row patterns.

4. Why this finds every solution, including the shortest

Soundness. After processing row r+1r+1r+1, row rrr matches the target. Later presses are too far away to affect it. By induction, the first m−1m-1m−1 rows are correct when the chase ends; the explicit last-row check establishes that the entire board is correct.

Completeness. Take any valid press pattern. Its first row is among the patterns we enumerate. Once that row is fixed, the parity equation forces its second row, then its third, and so on. Our chase therefore reconstructs that exact pattern. No solution is missed.

Minimum number of presses. A shortest action sequence never presses one cell twice, because removing both presses preserves the final board. Since the search covers every binary solution, retaining the valid pattern of smallest Hamming weight gives a global minimum under the rule that every press costs one:

p∗∈arg min⁡p valid∑r=1m∑c=1npr,c.p^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{p\text{ valid}} \sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{c=1}^{n}p_{r,c}. p∗∈p validargmin​r=1∑m​c=1∑n​pr,c​.

The sum here is an ordinary integer sum, not XOR. Stopping at the first valid candidate finds a solution, but does not in general establish that it is shortest. If no first-row candidate succeeds, the board is unsolvable under these rules.

6. A complete Python solver

The function accepts a rectangular list of rows and leaves its input unchanged. It returns a shortest list of zero-based coordinates; [] means the board is already solved, while None means there is no solution. Equal-length solutions are resolved by keeping the first one encountered.

def solve_lights(initial):
    """Return a shortest list of (row, col) presses, or None.

    Input: a nonempty rectangular 0/1 grid; 1 is the target state.
    Coordinates in the returned list are zero-based.
    """
    if not initial or not initial[0]:
        raise ValueError("The board must not be empty")
    rows, cols = len(initial), len(initial[0])
    if any(len(row) != cols for row in initial):
        raise ValueError("The board must be rectangular")
    if any(cell not in (0, 1) for row in initial for cell in row):
        raise ValueError("Each cell must be 0 or 1")

    best = None
    for mask in range(1 << cols):
        board = [list(row) for row in initial]
        presses = []

        def press(row, col):
            presses.append((row, col))
            for dr, dc in ((0, 0), (-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)):
                rr, cc = row + dr, col + dc
                if 0 <= rr < rows and 0 <= cc < cols:
                    board[rr][cc] ^= 1

        for col in range(cols):
            if mask & (1 << col):
                press(0, col)

        for row in range(1, rows):
            for col in range(cols):
                if board[row - 1][col] == 0:
                    press(row, col)

        if all(board[-1]):
            if best is None or len(presses) < len(best):
                best = presses

    return best

For the reset board in the table, the returned coordinates are [(0, 0), (0, 3), (1, 1), (2, 0), (2, 4), (3, 3)]. The bit at position col in mask corresponds to that column; column 0 is the leftmost cell even though ordinary binary numerals are usually printed with their least significant bit on the right.

7. Time and space

There are 2n2^n2n first-row choices. Each candidate copies an m×nm\times nm×n board and visits each cell a constant number of times; each press changes at most five cells. The implementation therefore uses

T(m,n)=O(mn 2n),S(m,n)=O(mn).\begin{aligned} T(m,n)&=O(mn\,2^n),\\ S(m,n)&=O(mn). \end{aligned} T(m,n)S(m,n)​=O(mn2n),=O(mn).​

Space includes the current board, its press list and the best list retained so far. On a 5×55\times55×5 board, the search processes only 32 candidates. For a long rectangle, transpose the board so that the enumerated row has the smaller dimension; after mapping the coordinates back, the time bound becomes O(mn 2min⁡(m,n))O(mn\,2^{\min(m,n)})O(mn2min(m,n)). This is still exponential in the smaller dimension, not a polynomial-time claim for arbitrary board sizes.

8. The linear-algebra view

Flatten the board into N=mnN=mnN=mn cells. Construct a binary matrix AAA with Aij=1A_{ij}=1Aij​=1 exactly when pressing cell jjj flips cell iii. It has ones on its diagonal and at orthogonal-neighbor positions. The parity constraints become

Ap=dover F2.Ap=d\quad\text{over }\mathbb F_2. Ap=dover F2​.

Gaussian elimination works here by XORing rows of the augmented matrix [A∣d][A\mid d][A∣d]. A row [0 ⋯ 0∣1][0\ \cdots\ 0\mid1][0 ⋯ 0∣1] proves inconsistency. Otherwise, set the free variables to obtain one solution p0p_0p0​, then describe all solutions with a basis of the kernel:

p=p0⊕⨁i=1kαivi,αi∈{0,1},k=N−rank⁡(A).\begin{aligned} p&=p_0\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_i v_i,\\ \alpha_i&\in\{0,1\},\\ k&=N-\operatorname{rank}(A). \end{aligned} pαi​k​=p0​⊕i=1⨁k​αi​vi​,∈{0,1},=N−rank(A).​

For the standard non-wrapping 5×55\times55×5 rule, AAA has rank 23 and nullity 2. A solvable board therefore has four binary press patterns; only one quarter of all starting boards can reach a fixed target. These are established properties of the classic puzzle [2].

Ordinary dense elimination takes O(N3)O(N^3)O(N3) binary field operations and O(N2)O(N^2)O(N2) storage. It finds feasibility and a solution, but does not automatically minimize the press count. One approach is to enumerate the 2k2^k2k kernel combinations and compare their Hamming weights. On this board k=2k=2k=2, so that comparison is small. The matrix model also extends to other interaction graphs; the rectangular chase proof specifically relies on the neighbor geometry used here [3].

9. What this means for the game

The rule, the solver and the presentation should agree about boundaries and the target color. A generator can guarantee solvability by applying legal presses to a solved board, as this page does. Choosing all 25 colors independently cannot give that guarantee. The number of scrambling presses is also not a reliable difficulty measure: presses can cancel, and another solution may be shorter.

For play, a useful hint can reveal the next forced press after a first-row choice, leaving the player to recognize the pattern. A solver is also useful for checking authored boards and comparing their minimum press counts. Those are applications of the analysis; the interactive board above is a small demonstration of the flip rule.

1. 先把题目定义清楚

点灯地板对应经典的 Lights Out（关灯问题）。项目指南已经介绍了下文使用的首行枚举方法 [1]。这里把它展开为一份完整的算法解析；文中的 Python 是用于解释规则的独立实现，并非游戏的 Unreal Engine 源码。

考虑一个 m×nm\times nm×n 的矩形棋盘。按下一格，会翻转它自身及上、下、左、右相邻格子的状态；不影响对角线，边界也不会首尾相连。因此，在本游戏的 5×55\times55×5 棋盘中，角落的一次操作影响 3 格，普通边缘影响 4 格，内部影响 5 格。

设初始状态为 sr,c∈{0,1}s_{r,c}\in\{0,1\}sr,c​∈{0,1}，青色为 111，橙色为 000。REMNANT 的目标是全部点亮。虽然经典题目通常要求全部熄灭，但只要把状态改写为“与目标的差异”，两者就有相同的求解形式：

dr,c=sr,c⊕tr,c,tr,c=1.d_{r,c}=s_{r,c}\oplus t_{r,c},\qquad t_{r,c}=1. dr,c​=sr,c​⊕tr,c​,tr,c​=1.

⊕\oplus⊕ 表示异或，也就是模 2 加法。差异为 111，说明该格需要被翻转奇数次；差异为 000，说明需要偶数次，零次也算。题目首先要求找到一组操作，消除所有差异；进一步还可以要求总按键次数最少。

2. 从操作序列变成二元决策

同一格按两次会抵消，操作顺序也不影响最终结果。这来自异或的结合律与交换律：

a⊕1⊕1=a,a⊕b⊕c=a⊕c⊕b.\begin{aligned} a\oplus1\oplus1&=a,\\ a\oplus b\oplus c&=a\oplus c\oplus b. \end{aligned} a⊕1⊕1a⊕b⊕c​=a,=a⊕c⊕b.​

因此，用 pr,c∈{0,1}p_{r,c}\in\{0,1\}pr,c​∈{0,1} 表示这一格“按一次”或“不按”就足够了，无需枚举按键顺序或重复操作。最终状态为：

sr,c′=sr,c⊕pr,c⊕pr−1,c⊕pr+1,c⊕pr,c−1⊕pr,c+1.\begin{aligned} s'_{r,c}={}&s_{r,c}\oplus p_{r,c}\oplus p_{r-1,c}\\ &\oplus p_{r+1,c}\oplus p_{r,c-1}\oplus p_{r,c+1}. \end{aligned} sr,c′​=​sr,c​⊕pr,c​⊕pr−1,c​⊕pr+1,c​⊕pr,c−1​⊕pr,c+1​.​

所有棋盘外的按键变量都视为零。令 sr,c′=tr,cs'_{r,c}=t_{r,c}sr,c′​=tr,c​，就得到每个格子必须满足的奇偶约束。直接枚举仍然需要考虑 2mn2^{mn}2mn 种按键方案；对于 5×55\times55×5 棋盘，就是 225=33,554,4322^{25}=33{,}554{,}432225=33,554,432 种。下一步利用相邻关系，进一步缩小搜索范围。

3. 枚举第一行，其余各行由递推确定

先确定第一行按哪些格子，再检查第一行的状态。如果某一格仍为橙色，就必须按下第二行同一列的格子。在尚未决定的后续行中，只有它还能改变上方这一格；第三行及更低位置的操作都够不到第一行。

于是，用第二行修正第一行，用第三行修正第二行，依次向下。这种方法通常称为 light chasing（逐行追灯）。用初始差异和按键变量表示，递推式为：

pr+1,c=dr,c⊕pr−1,c⊕pr,c⊕pr,c−1⊕pr,c+1,r<m.\begin{aligned} p_{r+1,c}={}&d_{r,c}\oplus p_{r-1,c}\oplus p_{r,c}\\ &\oplus p_{r,c-1}\oplus p_{r,c+1},\qquad r<m. \end{aligned} pr+1,c​=​dr,c​⊕pr−1,c​⊕pr,c​⊕pr,c−1​⊕pr,c+1​,r<m.​

边界外仍视为零，包括 p0,c=0p_{0,c}=0p0,c​=0。注意，这个式子里的 ddd 是初始差异。如果代码直接维护按键后的当前棋盘，等价规则就更简单：上方格子当前为橙色时，按它正下方的格子。

第 mmm 行下面已经没有下一行，所以追到最后时，末行必须恰好全部达到目标。等价地，对每一列都应满足：

dm,c⊕pm−1,c⊕pm,c⊕pm,c−1⊕pm,c+1=0.\begin{aligned} &d_{m,c}\oplus p_{m-1,c}\oplus p_{m,c}\\ &\qquad\oplus p_{m,c-1}\oplus p_{m,c+1}=0. \end{aligned} ​dm,c​⊕pm−1,c​⊕pm,c​⊕pm,c−1​⊕pm,c+1​=0.​

末行检查不通过，就说明这组首行选择失败。此时不能再随意按末行来“补救”，因为它会破坏已经修好的上一行。对于五列棋盘，只需尝试 25=322^5=3225=32 种首行方案。

4. 为什么不会漏解，为什么能找到最优解

正确性。 处理完第 r+1r+1r+1 行后，第 rrr 行已经符合目标。之后的操作距离太远，不会再影响它。依此归纳，追灯结束时前 m−1m-1m−1 行均已正确；再通过末行检查，就能确认整个棋盘已完成。

完备性。 任取一个合法解，它的首行方案必然在我们的枚举范围内。一旦首行固定，第二行由奇偶约束唯一确定，第三行也随之唯一确定，依此类推。因此，逐行递推必然重建出这个解，不会漏掉任何合法的二元按键方案。

最少按键次数。 最短操作序列不会在同一格按两次，因为同时去掉这两次操作，最终状态不变。既然搜索覆盖了所有二元解，只要保留其中汉明重量最小的方案，就得到了每次按键代价均为 1 时的全局最优解：

p∗∈arg min⁡p valid∑r=1m∑c=1npr,c.p^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{p\text{ valid}} \sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{c=1}^{n}p_{r,c}. p∗∈p validargmin​r=1∑m​c=1∑n​pr,c​.

这里使用普通整数加法统计次数，不是异或。找到第一组合法方案就停止，只能保证“有解”，通常不能保证“最少”。如果所有首行方案都无法通过末行检查，这个初始棋盘在当前规则下就是无解的。

先重置上方的互动棋盘。下表中的五位数字按一行的从左到右排列。初始棋盘从全亮状态出发，依次按下 (1,1),(1,4),(2,2),(3,1),(3,5),(4,4)(1,1),(1,4),(2,2),(3,1),(3,5),(4,4)(1,1),(1,4),(2,2),(3,1),(3,5),(4,4) 得到；此处坐标从 1 开始编号。再按一次这六个位置，一定能恢复全亮，因此这个示例也一定有解。

尝试首行方案 10010。操作后第一行变成 10111，所以第二行必须按第 2 列，即 01000。沿着相同逻辑向下推，便得到表中的各行方案，最终末行是 11111。遍历全部首行候选还能确认：这组 6 次按键的解就是最短解。表中的每一行记录“按哪些格子”，并非棋盘随时间变化的逐帧画面。

6. 完整的 Python 实现

函数接受一个由若干行组成的矩形列表，不会修改输入。返回值是最短解的从零开始编号的坐标列表；[] 表示初始棋盘已经完成，None 表示无解。若多个最短解的长度相同，保留最先找到的那一个。

def solve_lights(initial):
    """Return a shortest list of (row, col) presses, or None.

    Input: a nonempty rectangular 0/1 grid; 1 is the target state.
    Coordinates in the returned list are zero-based.
    """
    if not initial or not initial[0]:
        raise ValueError("The board must not be empty")
    rows, cols = len(initial), len(initial[0])
    if any(len(row) != cols for row in initial):
        raise ValueError("The board must be rectangular")
    if any(cell not in (0, 1) for row in initial for cell in row):
        raise ValueError("Each cell must be 0 or 1")

    best = None
    for mask in range(1 << cols):
        board = [list(row) for row in initial]
        presses = []

        def press(row, col):
            presses.append((row, col))
            for dr, dc in ((0, 0), (-1, 0), (1, 0), (0, -1), (0, 1)):
                rr, cc = row + dr, col + dc
                if 0 <= rr < rows and 0 <= cc < cols:
                    board[rr][cc] ^= 1

        for col in range(cols):
            if mask & (1 << col):
                press(0, col)

        for row in range(1, rows):
            for col in range(cols):
                if board[row - 1][col] == 0:
                    press(row, col)

        if all(board[-1]):
            if best is None or len(presses) < len(best):
                best = presses

    return best

对于表中的重置棋盘，返回结果为 [(0, 0), (0, 3), (1, 1), (2, 0), (2, 4), (3, 3)]。代码中 mask 的第 col 位对应第 col 列，第 0 列在棋盘最左侧；它与通常把最低有效位写在最右侧的二进制数字显示习惯不同，阅读时需要区分。

7. 时间与空间复杂度

首行共有 2n2^n2n 种选择。每次尝试需要复制一个 m×nm\times nm×n 棋盘，并对每个格子做常数次检查；一次按键最多改变五格。因此，这份实现的复杂度是：

T(m,n)=O(mn 2n),S(m,n)=O(mn).\begin{aligned} T(m,n)&=O(mn\,2^n),\\ S(m,n)&=O(mn). \end{aligned} T(m,n)S(m,n)​=O(mn2n),=O(mn).​

空间包括当前棋盘、本次操作列表和保留的最优列表。对 5×55\times55×5 棋盘来说，只需要处理 32 个候选。若棋盘是长矩形，可以先转置，让较短的一边作为被枚举的行宽，最后再把坐标映射回来，时间复杂度就变为 O(mn 2min⁡(m,n))O(mn\,2^{\min(m,n)})O(mn2min(m,n))。它对较小维度仍是指数复杂度，不能据此宣称任意规模都能在多项式时间内求出最短解。

8. 换一个角度：二元域上的线性方程组

把棋盘按行展开成 N=mnN=mnN=mn 个格子。构造二元矩阵 AAA：当按下第 jjj 格会翻转第 iii 格时，令 Aij=1A_{ij}=1Aij​=1，否则为零。其主对角线和对应正交相邻关系的位置为 1。所有奇偶约束就组成：

Ap=dover F2.Ap=d\quad\text{over }\mathbb F_2. Ap=dover F2​.

在增广矩阵 [A∣d][A\mid d][A∣d] 上做高斯消元，行相加使用异或。如果出现 [0 ⋯ 0∣1][0\ \cdots\ 0\mid1][0 ⋯ 0∣1]，方程组矛盾，说明无解。否则，给自由变量赋值可得到一个特解 p0p_0p0​；再求出核空间的一组基，就能表达所有解：

p=p0⊕⨁i=1kαivi,αi∈{0,1},k=N−rank⁡(A).\begin{aligned} p&=p_0\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_i v_i,\\ \alpha_i&\in\{0,1\},\\ k&=N-\operatorname{rank}(A). \end{aligned} pαi​k​=p0​⊕i=1⨁k​αi​vi​,∈{0,1},=N−rank(A).​

对于标准的、边界不相连的 5×55\times55×5 规则，矩阵 AAA 的秩为 23，核空间维数为 2。因此，每个有解棋盘都有四种二元按键方案；对于任一固定目标，只有四分之一的初始棋盘能到达它。这些是经典 Lights Out 已有的数学性质 [2]。

普通稠密高斯消元需要 O(N3)O(N^3)O(N3) 次二元域运算和 O(N2)O(N^2)O(N2) 空间。它能判断是否有解并给出一个解，但不会自动给出最少按键方案。一种做法是再枚举 2k2^k2k 种核空间组合，比较汉明重量。本题中 k=2k=2k=2，这一步很小。矩阵模型还能推广到其他交互图；前面的逐行递推证明则依赖这里特定的相邻结构 [3]。

9. 回到游戏设计

交互规则、求解器和视觉提示必须对边界与目标颜色有一致的定义。从已完成棋盘出发施加合法操作，可以保证生成有解关卡，本页示例就是如此。独立随机设置 25 个格子的颜色则没有这一保证。打乱时的按键次数也不能直接当作难度：操作可能相互抵消，还可能存在更短的另一组解。

在游玩中，可以在玩家选定首行后，提示下一步被约束确定的位置，让玩家继续观察规律。求解器也可以用于校验人工设计的棋盘、比较它们的最少按键次数。这些是算法分析可以支持的设计方法；上方互动棋盘主要用于直观展示翻转规则。

Using the room with your hands

Level 3 also supports Vive VR interaction. Players can grab, rotate, connect and trigger objects with tracked controllers. The puzzle system validates those actions against the same rules used for desktop interaction. A physical gesture changes a puzzle state only when the connection or action is valid.

We also built recovery into the space. Trigger volumes below the platforms reset NOVA's position and orientation after a fall, so an unsuccessful jump does not leave the player stranded beneath the room. Restored circuits open doors or remove barriers, giving each solution a visible consequence beyond its interface.

The laboratory's exit checks both completed puzzles and the memory fragment. That joins the two reasons to explore: repairing the facility and recovering the story hidden inside it.

Movement changes how the story feels

Grappling pulls the player toward an anchor while accounting for velocity, gravity and collisions. Flight gives the player control over pitch and momentum, with boosts and updrafts modifying motion. The feedback matters because the player needs to feel the consequences of an input quickly enough to plan the next one.

The Judicator encounter changes the demands again. Its floating eye can fire a laser, while sprint attacks and summoned robots shift the player's attention between evasion and combat. Vulnerability phases create opportunities to act. These changes in pressure give the encounter its rhythm.

Driving supports both joystick and gyroscope steering. We separated the assistance into heading correction with a PD controller and speed regulation with a PID controller. Different camera perspectives and a minimal interface leave space to read the landscape on the way to the laboratory.

Keeping the journey coherent

The game uses Blueprints for much of its flow and C++ for core systems. We organized progression around story, level and local interaction states. Solving a puzzle generates an event, and the game checks the relevant conditions before changing the story or loading another level.

For example, leaving the laboratory requires both its puzzles and the memory fragment. Save data records completed puzzles, collected fragments, the player's choice and the last checkpoint. Those details matter when a game keeps changing its mechanics: the player's progress has to survive the transition.

An experiment in boss decisions

Another direction was to learn boss behavior from combat footage. We explored recognizing and imitating actions from recorded encounters, then using a world model to score candidate moves by hit rate, risk and positional gain. We presented this as exploratory work. Evaluating how those decisions affect the experience of a fight is a further question.

What we looked for in playtesting

We collected 79 questionnaire responses and demonstrated the game at the Shandong Science and Technology Festival and a library showcase. The evaluation considered usability, engagement, immersion and interaction clarity. Our testing plan also called for recording confusion points, task completion times and difficulty with the controls.

For a game built around discovering rules, confusion needs careful interpretation. A player may be thinking through a puzzle, or may simply have missed the clue that explains how to interact. Our map design puts hints before the maze and keeps destinations visible so that players can spend more of their attention on the problem itself.

The questionnaires describe players' experience of the game. Measuring how much computer science they learned would require a separate study. Our design goal here was to make the ideas available through play: a route that can be reasoned about, a circuit that can be tested, and a pattern of lights whose behavior eventually makes sense.
