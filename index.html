Designing REMNANT

Echoes of Memory

How we connected computational puzzles, six changes of pace and a robot's search for human memory.

In REMNANT, we put a robot in a world that has survived its people. NOVA follows a program to rebuild Earth, while scattered memories gradually reveal what happened to humanity. The game asks what a machine should preserve when it reconstructs a human world.

We also wanted players to encounter computer science through action. A broken facility gave us a setting for that: routing signals, interpreting binary clues and changing the state of a room all become ways to move the story forward. The resulting game has six chapters, with a different kind of interaction in each.

A machine's orders, a human's memories

The story follows two lines. The machine side offers structure: an assistant provides hints, tasks state an objective, and driving assistance can help the player follow a route. These systems make order useful. The player has reasons to trust them.

Memory fragments complicate that trust. They reveal NOVA's identity and the human experiences hidden inside the robot's memory. Searching for them gives players a reason to explore beyond the obvious route. Progress through the world and recovery of the past happen together.

The final decision brings the two lines into conflict. Restoring human memories preserves emotion and the freedom to choose. Continuing as the Judicator prioritizes order and the reconstruction of Earth. We wanted the earlier interactions to give that decision some weight.

The two endings (spoilers)

Six chapters, six changes of pace

The changes in movement are part of the narrative rhythm. Driving gives the player time to look around. The laboratory slows the pace and asks for careful reasoning. Grappling and combat then demand quicker decisions. Flight opens up the space again, giving the memory-restoration ending a sense of release.

Building the environment

The laboratory shows how one of REMNANT's environments took shape. We began with a floor plan, built the rooms in simple geometry, then added the structures, materials and lighting that give the facility its character. The development captures below show both the overall layout and the changes inside one room.

Planning the rooms and routes

The 2D draft establishes a central meeting point, branches for the puzzle rooms and an exit toward the next chapter. Drawing these connections first lets us think about how a player moves between activities before committing to detailed geometry.

Those lines become corridors, room volumes and maze walls in the blockout. Plain surfaces and checkerboard grids keep the structure easy to inspect. At this stage, the useful questions are spatial: how rooms connect, how much space a platform offers, and where a change in height interrupts the route.

Assembling the facility, room by room

Using that structure as a scaffold, we assembled corridor sections, framed floors and mechanical walls. The intermediate view still has unfinished white rooms beside dressed corridors. It records the gradual replacement of the test geometry, with the network of rooms remaining recognizable throughout.

In the later view, the warm circular hub and the cyan light matrix form clear landmarks. Repeated wall and floor panels connect the rooms visually, while the machinery and lighting distinguish their functions. The facility gains detail without losing the outline established by the plan.

Materials and light inside the pipe chamber

The pipe chamber gives a closer comparison. Its blockout already contains stepped platforms, wall-mounted valves and a raised exit. The finished pass adds metal frames, panel seams and surface detail to these forms. Cyan energy below the platforms separates the lower area from the solid surfaces that carry the route.

Keeping the route legible

Glass partitions reveal the destination while the actual route takes a detour. The two-floor structure also lets us create a shortcut: after solving a puzzle, the player can drop from the upper level to the lower one. The space becomes easier to traverse as the player understands it. We placed the binary hint before the maze entrance, where it can inform the next decision.

The room also needs to show what a solution changes. Restoring a circuit opens a door or removes a barrier, so progress on a puzzle becomes a visible change in the environment. This connects the layout, its visual cues and the player's actions.

Inside Level 3: the laboratory

One of REMNANT's distinctive features is its laboratory chapter, where computer science concepts become puzzles in a 3D space. Five puzzle areas ask the player to reason about different kinds of systems: a connected path, competing routes, a signal network, a maze with conditional doors, and a floor of interacting binary states. Solving them restores the facility and changes where NOVA can go.

A facility with a readable layout

The central hub gives players a familiar place to return to between puzzle rooms. Each branch has its own visual identity, so a player can recognize the light floor or the pipe chamber before opening a puzzle interface. The environment study above follows the construction of this layout.

01 · Pipes: finding the path that carries power

The pipe board has 25 cells. Players rotate segments to carry power from the input to the output, then use Enable Circuit to test the route. Some pieces are decoys: making them look connected contributes nothing to the powered path.

That distinction gives the puzzle its purpose. The player has to trace which components belong to the working system and check their orientation. Local connections only help when they form a continuous route. Completing the circuit unlocks the physical way forward in the chamber, tying the result on the panel to movement through the room.

02 · Wires: several routes sharing one board

The wire puzzle asks players to connect matching colored terminals across a limited grid. A route that works on its own can occupy the only corridor another wire needs. Players often have to reconsider an earlier connection to make the whole layout work.

We drew on the layout problem in printed circuit boards. Every required connection has to coexist with the others. The interface tracks progress through successive routing stages; glowing paths show completed connections, and Reset lets the player try another arrangement. Completing the connections drives the room's door-opening animation.

03 · Logic gates: building a valid signal network

Our gate puzzle combines three signal states, red, blue and white, with a limited inventory of five operations. Players arrange gates and wires in columns. Signals move from left to right through every column; an empty cell can carry a wire, while an occupied gate cell blocks unrelated routes.

We implemented the gate logic in C++. Run checks the target colors together with the wiring rules. It rejects a two-input gate with only one input, a wire that skips a column, or a SPLIT output left unconnected. Every generated signal must have somewhere to go. Once the circuit passes validation, the laser barrier clears.

The five operations and the signal-count constraint
Examples from the game's three-color gate rules
GatePortsExample
RED2 in, 1 outR + B → R
BLUE2 in, 1 outR + B → B
CROSS2 in, 1 outR + B → W
SWAP1 in, 1 outR → B; W → W
SPLIT1 in, 2 outW → R + B

SPLIT is the only legal way to branch a signal. A gate with two inputs consumes two signals and produces one; SWAP leaves the count unchanged. With no unused outputs, a valid board must satisfy:

Targets = sources + SPLIT gates − two-input gates

Two sources and three targets therefore require one more SPLIT than merging gate. This gives the player a way to rule out an impossible design before routing every wire. The remaining task is to achieve the target colors within the available columns and gate inventory.

04 · The maze: decoding an instruction, then judging a door

A binary message on the wall becomes an ordered sequence of door numbers. The example route in our guide is 2 → 1 → 3 → 2 → 3. Entering the maze turns that decoded instruction into a spatial problem: the player has to find a usable door for each step.

We gave the doors six behaviors. They make the player check whether a route is actually available, and from which direction. A room can be understood as a graph node; a door adds an edge with a condition, a direction or a changing label.

Real
Opens or disappears as the player approaches.
Fake
Shows a number but does not open.
Number-changing
Updates its label when approached or used.
Double-numbered
Displays a different number on each side.
One-way
Allows passage in only one direction.
Two-way
Lets the player return and recheck a junction.

The clue supplies an instruction sequence; observation decides how to carry it out. A matching number on a fake door still leaves the player looking for a valid passage. That is where binary decoding meets conditional reasoning and graph traversal.

05 · Lights Out: an algorithm you can walk into

In the light room, the player operates a floor of 25 blocks. Pressing one reverses its state and the states of its horizontal and vertical neighbors. Correcting an orange block can undo a nearby cyan one, so each move has to account for the surrounding pattern.

We map the blocks' 3D positions to their grid coordinates and broadcast state changes to the affected neighbors. The immediate change in color lets the player test the rule in place. Cyan is the target state; restoring all 25 panels completes the mechanism.

This is a classic algorithmic puzzle: each press is a binary decision, and the local flip rule allows a small search over the first row to determine the entire solution. Our contribution is its place in the laboratory: a familiar algorithm becomes a floor the player can explore, test and restore.

Using the room with your hands

Level 3 also supports Vive VR interaction. Players can grab, rotate, connect and trigger objects with tracked controllers. The puzzle system validates those actions against the same rules used for desktop interaction. A physical gesture changes a puzzle state only when the connection or action is valid.

We also built recovery into the space. Trigger volumes below the platforms reset NOVA's position and orientation after a fall, so an unsuccessful jump does not leave the player stranded beneath the room. Restored circuits open doors or remove barriers, giving each solution a visible consequence beyond its interface.

The laboratory's exit checks both completed puzzles and the memory fragment. That joins the two reasons to explore: repairing the facility and recovering the story hidden inside it.

Movement changes how the story feels

Grappling pulls the player toward an anchor while accounting for velocity, gravity and collisions. Flight gives the player control over pitch and momentum, with boosts and updrafts modifying motion. The feedback matters because the player needs to feel the consequences of an input quickly enough to plan the next one.

The Judicator encounter changes the demands again. Its floating eye can fire a laser, while sprint attacks and summoned robots shift the player's attention between evasion and combat. Vulnerability phases create opportunities to act. These changes in pressure give the encounter its rhythm.

Driving supports both joystick and gyroscope steering. We separated the assistance into heading correction with a PD controller and speed regulation with a PID controller. Different camera perspectives and a minimal interface leave space to read the landscape on the way to the laboratory.

Keeping the journey coherent

The game uses Blueprints for much of its flow and C++ for core systems. We organized progression around story, level and local interaction states. Solving a puzzle generates an event, and the game checks the relevant conditions before changing the story or loading another level.

For example, leaving the laboratory requires both its puzzles and the memory fragment. Save data records completed puzzles, collected fragments, the player's choice and the last checkpoint. Those details matter when a game keeps changing its mechanics: the player's progress has to survive the transition.

An experiment in boss decisions

Another direction was to learn boss behavior from combat footage. We explored recognizing and imitating actions from recorded encounters, then using a world model to score candidate moves by hit rate, risk and positional gain. We presented this as exploratory work. Evaluating how those decisions affect the experience of a fight is a further question.

What we looked for in playtesting

We collected 79 questionnaire responses and demonstrated the game at the Shandong Science and Technology Festival and a library showcase. The evaluation considered usability, engagement, immersion and interaction clarity. Our testing plan also called for recording confusion points, task completion times and difficulty with the controls.

For a game built around discovering rules, confusion needs careful interpretation. A player may be thinking through a puzzle, or may simply have missed the clue that explains how to interact. Our map design puts hints before the maze and keeps destinations visible so that players can spend more of their attention on the problem itself.

The questionnaires describe players' experience of the game. Measuring how much computer science they learned would require a separate study. Our design goal here was to make the ideas available through play: a route that can be reasoned about, a circuit that can be tested, and a pattern of lights whose behavior eventually makes sense.
